              bounds only rounding contribution while ignoring under昀氀ow when classifying tiles for di昀昀erent IEEE 昀氀oating point precisions. Direct

              1 Introduction
              Mixed-precision computation has gained signi昀椀cant traction in machine learning due to its potential to reduce compu-
              tation time, memory footprint, and data movement. Block-scaled formats, which share an exponent across a group
              of elements, have emerged as an e昀昀ective low-precision representation for machine learning, supported in modern
              hardwareaccelerators. The Microscaling (MX) speci昀椀cation [19]4now supported on NVIDIA’s B200, AMD’s MI300, and
              Microsoft’s Maia 1004standardizes one such design point with a 昀椀xed 32-element block and an 8-bit shared scale across
              a family of sub-byte element formats (FP8, FP6, FP4), making block-scaled arithmetic broadly portable across vendors.
              Scienti昀椀c computing applications have been slower to adopt these techniques, often relying on double-precision arith-
              metic to preserve numerical stability. Showcased in recent Gordon Bell campaigns [3, 13], tile-centric mixed-precision

                Cholesky factorization [17] mitigates the cost of full double precision by classifying matrix tiles into precision buckets
                (e.g., FP32, FP16, FP8) with a norm-based rounding-error test. The classi昀椀cation rule is a single-threshold test: a tile is
                admitted to a bucket if its estimated rounding error lies below a user-speci昀椀ed tolerance ÿ. The assignment is made
                once, before factorization begins, and is not revisited during execution. This formulation is well suited to IEEE single
                precision, where the gap between the smallest normal number and typical factorization intermediates is large enough
                that rounding error dominates. The gap narrows at FP16 and shrinks by several orders of magnitude at FP84the
                lowest-precision bucket the baseline admits4where a non-negligible fraction of tile entries may silently under昀氀ow. The
                resulting error is not proportional to the unit roundo昀昀 and is therefore invisible to the rounding-only rule: in practice,
                the baseline can deliver a relative factorization error more than an order of magnitude (up to ∼60×) greater than the
                user’s requested ÿ while reporting that the assignment was successful, so the user-speci昀椀ed tolerance functions as a
                heuristic guideline rather than an enforced bound. Existing tile-centric mixed-precision frameworks also do not exploit
                block-scaled representations within tiles, even though such representations can substantially reduce the under昀氀ow
                componentoftheerrorandtherebyadmitlower-precision tiles than the IEEE-only candidate set allows.
                  Weaddressbothlimitations together via an error-controlled ladder for tile-centric precision assignment that natively
                accommodates block-scaled formats as candidates. The choice to fold scaled representations into the ladder’s candidate
                set rather than evaluate them in isolation is deliberate: applying a more principled assignment rule to the baseline’s
                IEEE precision tiers tightens the error bound but loses throughput, because both FP8 and FP16 tiles are pushed up to
                FP32oncetheirunder昀氀owcontributionsbecomevisible4FP32becomestheonlyIEEEcandidatethatconsistentlypasses
                the admissibility test for tiles the lower tiers can no longer carry. Scale-aware candidates close this gap4the shared
                scale enlarges the e昀昀ective dynamic range at sub-byte and narrow-IEEE precision alike, letting the ladder admit tiles at
                the low tiers again with provable bounds on both rounding and under昀氀ow. The ladder and the scaled candidates are
                therefore complementary by design: the ladder is what makes scaled candidates safe to deploy, and scaled candidates
                are what make the ladder fast enough to be useful.
                  Theladder walks candidate precisions from cheapest to most expensive and, at each tier, bounds both the rounding
                error (via the classical norm-based inequality) and the under昀氀ow error (via an entrywise scan against the smallest
                representable value of the candidate format); the 昀椀rst precision whose combined bound satis昀椀es the user tolerance
                is selected. The ladder is format-agnostic and natively accommodates per-block scaling factors, treating IEEE and
                block-scaled formats as peer candidates on the same admissibility test, with the standardized MX format (ġ=32, 8-bit
                shared scale) as a 昀椀rst-class member of the candidate set. We evaluate three instantiations to attribute observed gains to
                the admissibility rule, the on-hardware MX candidates, and the paper-proposed MXFP16 candidate: (i) Ladder IEEE
                (FP8, FP16, FP32, FP64) restricted to the baseline’s precision tiers; (ii) Ladder MX (MXFP4, MXFP8, FP16, FP32, FP64)
                that adds the OCP-standardized sub-byte MX candidates supported by native Blackwell MMAs; and (iii) Ladder MX +
                MXFP16 (MXFP4, MXFP8, MXFP16, FP32, FP64), where MXFP16 is our extension of the OCP MX block-scaled design to
                16-bit elements, executed via TF32 tensor cores.

                scienti昀椀c applications such as climate modeling, on a single NVIDIA GB200 GPU demonstrate that the ladder tracks

                this accuracy guarantee: Ladder IEEE pays for it in throughput, since both FP8 and FP16 tiles are promoted to FP32 once
                the under昀氀ow contribution becomes visible, eliminating the cheap tiers the baseline relied on; Ladder MX recovers

                    2 BackgroundandBaselineFramework
                    2.1  MixedPrecisioninDenseLinearAlgebra
                    Mixed-precision algorithms have long been studied in high-performance computing (HPC) as a mechanism to reduce
                    memoryfootprint and increase arithmetic throughput while preserving numerical accuracy. Two recent surveys frame
                    the 昀椀eld: Abdelfattah et al. [1] review numerical linear algebra methods utilizing mixed-precision arithmetic across
                    sparse and dense workloads, while Higham and Mary [11] provide a uni昀椀ed theoretical treatment of mixed-precision
                    algorithms with an emphasis on rounding-error analysis and the conditions under which low-precision storage and
                    computation are numerically safe. Building on this framework, Mary and Mikaitis [14] extend the analysis to narrow-
                    rangelow-precisionformats(FP8andbelow,downtoFP4),wherethesmalldynamicrangemakesunder昀氀owa昀椀rst-order
                    concern alongside rounding; their disjoint rounding-plus-under昀氀ow decomposition forms the basis of our tile-centric
                    admissibility criterion in Section 3.1.
                      Several works have investigated mixed precision in core linear algebra kernels. Zhang et al. [22] study GEMM
                    operations using double and single precision in hardware-aware settings. Reliability aspects of mixed precision across
                    HPCworkloads are analyzed by Netti et al. [15], who note that half precision remains relatively underutilized in
                    scienti昀椀c applications compared to single and double precision. GPU-oriented optimizations for linear solvers using
                    mixedprecision,includingiterativere昀椀nementstrategieswithFP16,areexploredbyHaidaretal.[10].TheuseofTensor
                    Cores for accelerating Cholesky factorization in half precision is investigated by Abdelfattah et al. [2], demonstrating
                    performance gains while maintaining acceptable numerical stability through re昀椀nement techniques.
                    2.2  Tile-Based Precision Assignment in Cholesky
                    Tile-based decompositions enable 昀椀ne-grained precision assignment, allowing di昀昀erent tiles to be stored and computed
                    at varying bit widths based on conditioning or error sensitivity. Two prior approaches are the most directly relevant
                    comparators for this work.
                      Closest in spirit, Carrica et al. [6] combine recursive Cholesky on NVIDIA and AMD matrix engines with a per-block
                    scalar rescaling around each FP16 GEMM. This anticipates block-scaled execution, but the scaling is an external scalar

                             wrapping an IEEE FP16 block rather than a hardware-portable format with a shared exponent applied at 昀椀xed-size
                             element blocks, and precision is assigned by recursion-tree position rather than a tile norm-based rounding-error test.

                             Its tile-classi昀椀cation rule applies a single-threshold test on rounding error against a user tolerance ÿ and is invariant
                             to the under昀氀ow contribution4a fragility that can let the actual factorization error exceed the requested ÿ by more
                             than an order of magnitude at loose tolerances where FP8 under昀氀ow is non-negligible. The error-controlled ladder is
                             designed to close this gap.
                                While prior mixed-precision approaches rely on IEEE formats (FP16, FP32, FP64)4and where scaling appears, it is a
                             single external scalar wrapping an IEEE block4none combine a per-tile rounding-plus-under昀氀ow admissibility rule
                             with hardware-portable block-scaled candidate formats in dense scienti昀椀c factorizations. This is the gap that our work
                             addresses.
                             2.3   Block-Scaled Formats
                             Sharing a 昀氀oating-point exponent across a group of values is a long-standing idea [21], with modern instantiations
                             including Microsoft’s MSFP [8] and Block Mini昀氀oat [9]. These schemes reduce storage and hardware complexity, but
                             a single shared exponent is sensitive to outliers: every element in a block must conform to it without per-element
                             adjustment. This sensitivity intensi昀椀es as element bit-widths shrink4from FP16’s tens of binades of dynamic range,
                             to FP8’s handful, to FP4’s mere few4making block-scaled construction not merely a memory optimization but a
                             representational necessity at sub-byte widths.
                                The Microscaling (MX) speci昀椀cation, an Open Compute Project (OCP) standard [18, 19], re昀椀nes this design by
                             combining a per-element microscale exponent with a shared 8-bit scale over 昀椀xed 32-element blocks, reducing clipping
                             risk and improving robustness to intra-block dynamic-range variation. Initial machine learning evaluations show that
                             sub-byte MX formats can replace FP32 with minimal accuracy loss.
                                Hardwaresupportnowspanscommercialaccelerators (NVIDIA Blackwell, AMD MI300) as well as FPGA datapath
                             designs for MX [5, 20] and ASIC precision-scalable MACs supporting the full MX family [7].
                                While MXhasbeenextensively evaluated in deep learning, its implications for structured dense scienti昀椀c computa-
                             tions remain largely unexplored. This work integrates MX as a 昀椀rst-class candidate in the precision-assignment ladder
                             of Section 3.1.
